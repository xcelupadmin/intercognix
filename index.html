The Unknown Between the Known

I.The Library Without Corridors

every fact is known; the relationships were never built

Picture everything humanity has written as one vast library. Medicine in one wing, physics in another; history, law, finance and literature each in its own hall, meticulously catalogued. Now notice what the library does not contain: corridors. A historian rarely reads an obscure pharmacology paper. A physicist rarely meets an observation buried in ancient poetry. No single mind has ever stood in two halls at once.

cf. Swanson, 19861 — fish oil & Raynaud's

That such corridors exist is not conjecture. In the 1980s the information scientist Don Swanson examined two bodies of medical literature that had developed independently — one on dietary fish oil, the other on Raynaud's phenomenon. Neither contained an answer. Considered together, they suggested a connection nobody had recognized, one that later clinical investigation supported.1 The clues had been in plain sight: not inside any single document, but between literatures that had barely spoken.

humans run out of lifetime

Swanson walked his corridor by hand, one literature at a time, in a library of millions of halls. Today we are converting human knowledge into embeddings — shared computational spaces in which ideas from utterly different disciplines become comparable at scales no individual could approach. For the first time, the corridors can be searched. All of them. At once.

the question this folio exists to ask

What is in there that nobody knows to ask about? Every prompt we type is bounded by our own imagination. We have built extraordinary machines for answering questions, and barely begun building machines that discover questions which do not yet exist.

II.The Shape of the Thing

A and B are old; the bridge is not

A is known. B is known. Nothing individually is new. But nobody has seen the bridge — and the moment it becomes visible, it implies something else. Call it X. It was never stored in A, never stored in B; possibly it was never written down in human history. Yet it becomes cleanly inferable the instant A and B are held in the same frame.

there was no word for X

What do we call X before anyone has proven it? It is not a forgotten fact awaiting retrieval, and it is emphatically not a machine's invention promoted to truth. It is a stranger thing: an implication of what humanity already knows, that humanity has never known. There was no word for that. So one was coined.

III.The Word

Intercognix n. · coined 2026

inter, between · cogni, knowing · x, the unknown

A candidate insight discoverable through previously unrecognized relationships among independently known things.

It is

  • a candidate — unproven until validated
  • an implication of what is already known
  • discoverable only through relationships

It is not

  • retrieval of a forgotten fact
  • a hallucination promoted to truth
  • knowledge, until it survives scrutiny

Candidate is the operative word. Most intercognix candidates will collapse under evidence, prior-art checks and expert scrutiny — and they should. The one in a million that survives becomes something extraordinary: a discovery nobody was looking for, exposed inside humanity's own accumulated knowledge.

IV.The Practice

relevance finds the expected; surprise finds the new

How do you search for something you don't know exists? You cannot ask for the answer — no one yet knows the question. Traditional search optimizes for relevance; this hunt optimizes for productive surprise. Carry the following instruction into your next session with an advanced language model:

"Find concepts from maximally distant disciplines that occupy unexpectedly nearby semantic territory. Ignore obvious relationships. For each unexpected proximity, determine what underlying principle might explain it. Then tell me what question becomes possible only when these concepts are considered together — and what evidence would validate or falsify it."

the changed relationship

Notice what the instruction does. It never asks the machine for an answer; it asks the machine to find something worth asking about. Don't just ask for answers. Ask what deserves a question. The corridors are unlit — and for the first time, we may be building a lantern that fits.

VI.The Engine

the instrument that found the specimens above is now running, and it will take your problem instead of ours

Every candidate in the catalogue was found the same way. Not by a reader who happened to hold two literatures at once — that is serendipity, and serendipity cannot be commissioned — but by a machine built to hold thousands of them at once, and to go looking on purpose. That machine now exists outside these pages.

An expedition begins where the work has stopped: a problem stated as unresolved phenomena rather than as a hypothesis waiting for support. The engine reads across domains that have never cited one another, proposes the bridges, and then turns on them — each candidate met with the prior art that may already have answered it, the evidence for and against, and the experiment that would break it. What returns is a short list that survived the attack, and a plain account of why the rest did not.
